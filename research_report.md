# Research Report

**Safeguarding Public Healthcare Spending with Explainable AI: A Statistically Validated Machine Learning Framework for Medicaid Fraud Detection and Electronic Visit Verification**

Md Sibbir Hossain · ORCID 0009-0002-0795-4512

> All results in this report were produced on **synthetic data with simulated labels**. Nothing here measures real-world Medicaid fraud detection performance. Figures were generated by `make all` at seed 20260913 and are reproducible from the repository.

## Abstract

Medicaid personal-care programs generate two parallel data streams — billing claims and Electronic Visit Verification (EVV) telemetry — that are rarely modelled jointly. We present an explainable framework that combines transparent rules, supervised learning, unsupervised anomaly detection and hybrid risk fusion to prioritise suspicious activity for human review, evaluated under time-based validation with explicit leakage control and probability calibration.

On a synthetic dataset of 45,297 claims with 43,106 linked EVV events at 3.97% simulated prevalence, the selected model (LightGBM) achieves PR-AUC 0.889 (caregiver-clustered bootstrap CI [0.860, 0.917]) against 0.354 for a transparent rule baseline. Feature ablation shows billing-only and EVV-only feature families reach PR-AUC 0.437 and 0.593 respectively, while their combination reaches 0.740 — a gain of 0.147 over the better half alone, which is the study's principal finding.

Two results run against the framework's motivating hypothesis and are reported as such. First, hybrid fusion did **not** outperform the best individual supervised model (0.865 vs 0.889; difference favouring the single model, CI [0.013, 0.037]). Second, the selected model is **not** statistically distinguishable from a random forest (ΔPR-AUC +0.010, CI [−0.002, 0.021]; DeLong *p* = 0.242); its practical advantage is a 10× reduction in training time. We further show that accuracy is uninformative at this prevalence: a constant-negative classifier scores 0.960 while every useful model spans 0.983–0.990.

**Keywords:** explainable AI, healthcare program integrity, anomaly detection, electronic visit verification, probability calibration, class imbalance, Medicaid

## 1. Introduction

Program integrity in Medicaid home and personal care rests on a documentation problem. A service is delivered in a private residence with no institutional witness; the evidence that it occurred is an EVV record, and the request for payment is a claim. When those two disagree, someone has to look.

The volume makes looking expensive. A mid-sized state program processes millions of personal-care claims annually against an investigative unit measured in tens of staff. The operational question is therefore not "is this claim fraudulent" but "which few thousand claims should a finite team examine first" — a ranking problem under a capacity constraint.

This work asks whether joint modelling of billing and EVV behaviour improves that ranking relative to transparent rules and individual models, and whether the result can be made explainable and calibrated enough to be defensible when an alert affects a caregiver's income.

## 2. Background and motivation

The 21st Century Cures Act requires states to implement EVV for Medicaid personal care and home health services. EVV captures check-in and check-out times, and where GPS verification is used, locations. This creates, for the first time at scale, a verification stream that can be compared against what was billed.

It also creates a fairness hazard. EVV completeness depends on smartphone ownership, connectivity and data-entry literacy. A model that treats missing verification as strong evidence of misconduct will systematically rank rural caregivers, small agencies and lower-income workers higher. Section 8 returns to this; it is why the missing-EVV rule carries a deliberately low weight.

## 3. Related work

Healthcare fraud detection literature concentrates on Medicare claims, where public provider-level files exist, and typically frames the task as supervised classification against provider-level labels of uncertain provenance. Three gaps motivate this work:

1. **EVV is essentially absent** from the published modelling literature, because the data is not public.
2. **Evaluation practice is weak.** Random splits on time-ordered claims, accuracy reported without base rate, and uncalibrated scores presented as probabilities are common.
3. **Explainability is usually retrofitted** as a SHAP plot rather than designed as reviewable evidence.

This work does not claim to beat the state of the art on a shared benchmark — no such benchmark exists for the EVV problem. It contributes a methodology and a reproducible implementation.

## 4. Public dataset selection

Documented fully in `reports/dataset_discovery.md`. Summary: no public dataset contains linked Medicaid personal-care claims, EVV telemetry and verified fraud labels, for structural reasons (EVV is operational PHI; fraud determinations are legal outcomes; public claims files are aggregated for re-identification safety). The HHS OIG LEIE and popular Kaggle provider-fraud datasets were examined and rejected as primary sources — the former because exclusion is not a claim-level fraud finding and postdates the conduct, the latter because label provenance and licensing are undocumented.

Consequence: the EVV track is synthetic, cross-dataset validation is not performed, and every performance claim is scoped to simulated labels.

## 5. Problem formulation

Let claim $c_i$ have features $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and label $y_i \in \{0,1\}$ indicating an injected suspicious scenario. Given review budget $K$, maximise

$$\frac{1}{K}\sum_{i \in \text{top-}K(s)} y_i$$

over scoring functions $s$. Reported metrics follow: PR-AUC as the threshold-free ranking summary, Precision@K and Recall@K at realistic capacities (1%, 5%, 10%), Brier and ECE for calibration, and workload in investigator-hours.

**Why not accuracy.** At prevalence $\pi$, the constant predictor achieves $1-\pi$ accuracy with zero recall. At $\pi = 0.0397$ that is 0.960. Every table in this work reports `accuracy` beside `accuracy_trivial_baseline` $=\max(\pi, 1-\pi)$ to force the comparison.

## 6. Dataset and synthetic methodology

The generator produces providers (60), caregivers (900), patients (2,200), authorisations (2,200), claims (45,297) and EVV events (43,106) over a simulated year, with persistent caregiver–patient assignments, heavy-tailed agency sizes, weekday-weighted bimodal shift starts, gamma-distributed authorised hours and realistic GPS jitter.

Normal behaviour is deliberately messy: irregular hours, 4% benign missing EVV, submission lags from days to weeks, non-GPS verification methods that legitimately carry no coordinates, and genuine duration variance. Sixteen suspicious scenarios are injected as *behavioural changes* — never as a flag column — covering overlapping visits, authorisation exceedance, implausible travel, duration mismatch, missing EVV, exact and near duplicates, billing spikes, excessive workload, provider concentration, after-hours and weekend activity, level shifts, batch-entry timestamp signatures, coordinated rings, and authorisation inconsistency.

**A generator defect found by the test suite and fixed.** Check-in and check-out offsets were initially drawn independently, which inverted 36% of EVV intervals on short visits — an artefact that would have placed a spurious signal on ordinary claims. The check-out offset is now drawn relative to check-in with a positive duration floor. The defect was caught by `test_schema_and_keys_are_clean` before any result was reported, which is the argument for validation tests over eyeballing summary statistics.

## 7. Feature engineering

101 features across five families: billing (44), EVV (36), temporal (15), robust-statistical (6), network (8). See `docs/methodology.md` §5.

**Leakage control** operates at three levels — column blocklist, strictly backward-looking temporal aggregates (`sort → groupby → shift(1) → rolling`), and train-window-only fitting of every learned statistic — with a diagnostic guard flagging any single feature at AUC ≥ 0.999. On the reported run: none flagged.

## 8. Proposed hybrid framework

Rules (noisy-OR over ten weighted severity ramps), four supervised learners with `class_weight="balanced"`, Isolation Forest and LOF, and two fusion strategies: a fixed convex combination and a logistic meta-learner fitted on validation. Isotonic calibration on the validation window is applied to every probabilistic score including the rule baseline, so the Brier comparison is fair.

## 9. Explainability methodology

Each alert carries rule evidence as plain sentences with severities, SHAP local attributions, the calibrated probability with its method, peer comparison, and an explicit interpretation boundary stating that attributions describe model behaviour and are not evidence of intent or wrongdoing. Permutation importance is provided as a model-agnostic cross-check.

## 10–11. Statistical validation and experimental setup

Percentile bootstrap (1,000 resamples), caregiver-clustered bootstrap (300), paired bootstrap on PR-AUC differences (400), DeLong for correlated ROC-AUCs, exact-binomial McNemar at the 5% threshold. Split: train ≤ 2024-08-31 (30,334 claims, 4.21% prevalence), validate ≤ 2024-10-31 (7,730), test thereafter (7,233, 3.97%).

### 12.2 The accuracy result

Accuracy spans 0.983–0.990 across models whose PR-AUC spans 0.788–0.889. The rule baseline, which is operationally useful, *underperforms* the do-nothing classifier on accuracy. Selecting on accuracy would return hybrid-weighted (0.990), fourth on PR-AUC. We regard this as the clearest single demonstration in the study of why imbalanced detection must not be evaluated on accuracy.

### 12.3 Significance

Gains over rules (+0.535), Isolation Forest (+0.679), LOF (+0.482) and logistic regression (+0.101) are large with intervals well clear of zero. The gain over random forest (+0.010, CI [−0.002, 0.021], DeLong *p* = 0.242) is **not** established. LightGBM's defensible advantage is efficiency: 5.6 s training versus 57.9 s (RF) and 79.3 s (GB).

### 12.4 Hypothesis 3 was not supported

Neither fusion strategy beat the best individual model (weighted 0.846, stacked 0.865, vs 0.889). The paired bootstrap favours the single model in both cases. We report this rather than re-tuning fusion weights until the result reverses, which would be selection on the test window. The hybrid's remaining justification is that it carries rule evidence usable in an appeal, and that it won at 2% and 6% prevalence in earlier configurations — a robustness property, not a ranking one.

| + network | 101 | 0.889 | +0.050 |

The joint billing+EVV gain is the study's principal positive finding. The robust-statistical block adds +0.003 once temporal features are present and should be dropped on parsimony grounds; it is retained in the repository only so the ablation remains reproducible.

## 14. Robustness and fairness

**Scenario generalisation.** Held-out scenarios recover at 0.900 (suspicious timestamps), 0.769 (coordinated activity) and 0.417 (near-duplicates) recall@5%, versus 1.000 for eight trained scenarios. Missing EVV recovers at 0.188 — appropriate, since a missing record is weak evidence.

**Class imbalance.** PR-AUC holds at 0.843–0.918 across 1%–6% injected prevalence. Precision@5% moves almost entirely with base rate (0.206 → 0.995), which is arithmetic, not model quality.

**Fairness.** Across synthetic cohorts PR-AUC ranges 0.865–0.932 and FPR 0.031–0.047. **These results describe randomly assigned synthetic cohorts and transfer to no real population.** Their purpose is to validate the measurement pipeline. Real deployment risks — documentation-capacity bias, technology-access bias, and reviewer feedback loops — are analysed in `docs/privacy.md`.

## 15. Computational efficiency

Single CPU core: feature engineering 3.62 s for 45,297 claims; rules 0.01 s; LightGBM 5.61 s training and 0.86 s inference on 7,233 claims. The pipeline is comfortably within the reach of a laptop or a Colab free instance, which matters for reproducibility by reviewers without institutional compute.

## 16. Operational impact simulation

`impact/simulator.py` converts measured ranking performance plus user-supplied assumptions into review workload and an illustrative avoided-loss range. At 5% capacity on 100,000 claims: 5,000 alerts, ~4.4 investigator FTE at 1.5 hours per review. **Every output is labelled "Illustrative scenario-based estimate—not measured real-world savings."** No dollar figure in this work is a measured saving, and the improper-payment rate is a user input, not a CMS statistic.

## 17. Limitations

See `reports/limitations.md`. The binding ones: synthetic labels; shared authorship of generator and detector; H3 unsupported; the winner not statistically separable from random forest; single simulated year; no cross-dataset validation; synthetic fairness cohorts.

## 18. Privacy and security

No real PHI is present or required. Entity identifiers are excluded from the feature matrix. The API exposes no endpoint that alters a claim's status. Full threat model in `SECURITY.md` and `docs/privacy.md`.

## 19. Conclusion

Joint billing–EVV modelling substantially outperforms either stream alone (0.740 vs 0.437 and 0.593 PR-AUC) and all models substantially outperform transparent rules (0.354). The framework's hybrid fusion did not beat the best single model, and the selected model is not statistically separable from a random forest — both reported as negative results. Accuracy was shown to be uninformative at this prevalence.

The contribution is methodological: a reproducible, leakage-controlled, calibrated evaluation harness for a problem where public data does not exist, together with an honest account of what such a harness can and cannot establish.

**Future work.** Evaluation on real EVV data under a state agency data-use agreement; a generator authored independently of the detector; multi-year drift; graph-native models for coordinated activity, which generalised worst among held-out scenarios; and a randomly sampled control stream to break reviewer feedback loops.
